The one-paragraph version

Take — big US companies. For each one, measure — things about how its price has been behaving lately — how much it has risen, how jumpy it is, how far it sits above its own average price, and so on. A model trained on — past examples turns those measurements into a guess: will this stock beat the market over the next — trading days? That guess is then blended with plain “what went up the most last month” (— model, — momentum). The top — become the portfolio, safety rules trim the position sizes, and the difference from what is already held becomes a list of orders.

What these results do not include

  • Trading costs beyond a small slippage allowance. No commissions, no taxes, no bid-ask spread beyond the 5 basis points assumed per trade.
  • Survivorship bias. The — companies were chosen today, already knowing which ones survived and thrived. Any strategy tested on today's winners looks better than it would have at the time.
  • Real-world fills. The simulation buys and sells at the closing price. Real orders do not get that price.
  • Repeated tuning. The settings were chosen by people who had already seen this data. Every extra knob turned makes the backtest prettier and the future less predictable.

How it works, assuming you know nothing about this

No finance background needed, no programming background needed. Every technical word is explained the first time it appears, and any underlined word like this can be clicked to jump to its glossary entry.

Three things to know before anything else

  1. The program knows nothing about the companies. It has never heard of iPhones or oil prices. It only sees a table of daily prices and how many shares changed hands. That is genuinely all.
  2. Being right about 51% of the time is the normal outcome here, not a bug. Predicting stocks is close to predicting coin flips. The entire question is whether a tiny edge, applied over and over, adds up.
  3. No real money is involved. This is paper trading: a broker account with pretend dollars in it that otherwise behaves like a real one. It is a research project, not advice.

The whole thing, in seven steps

  1. 1

    Collect the prices

    Once a week the program downloads the daily price history of — large American companies — Apple, JPMorgan, Exxon, Walmart, that sort of thing — plus one extra thing called SPY.

    What is SPY? A single fund that owns a slice of the 500 biggest US companies at once. Buying it is the lazy default: you get “the market” and never think again. Everything here is measured against SPY, because beating that lazy default is the only thing that would make picking stocks worth the effort.
    The full list of companies it chooses from

    This list was hand-picked and does not change week to week. That matters — see “survivorship bias” in the honest-assessment section below.

  2. 2

    Turn raw prices into clues

    A price on its own tells you nothing. “Apple costs $333” is not information; a company being worth more per share than another says nothing about which is the better buy. So for every company on every day, the program computes — numbers describing the shape of its recent price behaviour. In machine-learning language these are called features. In plain language, clues.

    Momentum

    How much has it risen over the last 1, 5, 20, 60 and 120 trading days? A stock up 20% in a month behaves differently from one that has gone nowhere.

    Volatility

    How jumpy is it? Two stocks can both end the month up 5% — one drifting calmly, one lurching ±4% a day. This measures the lurching.

    Distance from its moving average

    The moving average is just the average price over the last N days — a smoothed version of the squiggly line. Sitting 8% above your own 50-day average means you are on a run.

    RSI

    A 0–100 speedometer of how one-sided recent daily moves have been. Near 100 means almost every recent day was an up day.

    Drawdown

    How far below its own recent peak is it right now? A stock 2% off its high is in a different situation from one 30% off its high.

    Volume surprise

    Is today's trading activity unusual for this stock? Expressed as a z-score: “how many normal-sized steps away from its own normal is this?”

    The trick that makes these usable: ranking. Every clue is also converted into “where does this stock stand among the other — today?”, on a 0-to-1 scale. A score of 0.94 means “top 6% of the group”. This matters enormously: rising 5% in a month is impressive in a dead market and unimpressive in a booming one. Ranking removes the mood of the market and leaves the comparison.
  3. 3

    Show it history and let it learn

    Here is the actual machine learning, and it is simpler than it sounds. For every company on every past day, we already know how the story ended. So we write down the answer:

    The question being learned over the next — trading days, did this stock go up more than SPY did?  yes = 1  no = 0

    That yes/no answer is called the label. The program is handed — of these historical examples — — clues going in, one yes/no answer coming out — and told: find the patterns.

    What kind of learner is it? A gradient-boosted tree ensemble. Picture a very simple flowchart: “has it risen more than average? is it calm? → guess yes.” On its own that flowchart is bad. So you build a second flowchart whose only job is to fix the first one's mistakes. Then a third to fix what is still wrong. Repeat 450 times and add up all their votes. The committee is far better than any single member. That is the whole idea — no neural networks, no chatbots, nothing that “understands” anything.
    The rule that keeps it honest: the model only ever sees data from before —. Every result shown on this site happens after that date, on days the model has never laid eyes on. Testing a model on data it was trained on is marking your own homework, and it is the single most common way people fool themselves.
  4. 4

    Score today's stocks

    For each company today, the trained model produces a probability — say “0.58”, meaning it leans towards this one beating the market over the next week. But the model on its own is only barely better than a coin flip, so its opinion is deliberately diluted:

    Final score for each stock — × the model's ranking + — × the plain 20-day momentum ranking

    Read that again, because it is the most important sentence on this page: three quarters of the decision is simply “which stocks went up the most over the last month”, and one quarter is the model's opinion. The blend is set that way because plain momentum has historically worked about as well as anything cleverer — you can see that for yourself on the .

    • 6

      Turn targets into orders

      The program asks the broker what is currently held, compares it to the new targets, and trades only the difference. Anything no longer in the top — is sold completely. Anything already close enough to its target is left alone — adjustments smaller than — are skipped, because nudging a position by a few dollars is not worth a trade.

      Why the orders say PLANNED. The weekly automated run always stops at writing the order list down. Actually sending orders — even pretend ones — requires a person to trigger it deliberately, after reading a checklist. Placing a trade is the one step that cannot be undone, so it is the one step that is never automatic.
    • 7

      Check the homework three ways

      Building a strategy that looks spectacular on the past is genuinely easy and almost worthless. So there are three separate checks, in increasing order of how much you should trust them:

      Least trustworthy
      The backtest

      Replay — to today with a pretend $100,000, rebalancing every — days. One period, one result, and the settings were chosen by people who already knew what happened in it.

      More trustworthy
      The baselines

      Run three deliberately stupid rules through the exact same machinery. If “just buy whatever went up most” matches the model, then the model is decoration.

      Most trustworthy
      Walk-forward

      Train through 2021, test on 2022. Train through 2022, test on 2023. And so on. Each year is predicted knowing only what was knowable then — the closest honest imitation of really having run it.

    So is it any good? An honest answer

    The headline looks fantastic. In the backtest since — the strategy returned — while SPY returned —. Please do not stop reading here.

    The model itself is barely better than a coin flip. It gets — of its individual yes/no calls right. Fifty percent is a coin. That is not automatically fatal — a casino's edge is smaller than that — but it does mean no single pick on this site means very much.

    A one-line rule with no machine learning did about as well. Buying whatever rose the most over the previous month returned — in the same backtest. Since three quarters of the score is that rule, this is not a coincidence: the honest description of this project is “a momentum strategy with a machine-learning garnish”.

    And the honest test is much less flattering. Across — walk-forward years, the average return beyond SPY was —. It beat SPY in — of those — years, and at its worst moment the account was down — from its peak. Sitting through that in real life is much harder than reading about it.

    Fair summary: there is no evidence yet that the machine-learning part adds anything on top of plain momentum, and the walk-forward test suggests the whole thing has not reliably beaten simply owning SPY. That is a perfectly respectable result for a research project — it is what the checks are for — and it is exactly why nobody should put real money behind it.
